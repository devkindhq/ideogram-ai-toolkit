# Worked Example — Product Icon Concept Grid

Illustrative example — prompt shown was not actually run through Ideogram; format
matches this skill's real usage pattern.

A from-scratch fictional brand invented to demonstrate this skill end to end: a
bike-share app called "Spokewise," unrelated to any client or real product. Spokewise
needs a mark/icon direction before it can move into a full `brand-identity-sheet` —
there are six candidate motifs in play, and rather than generate six full identity
sheets, this skill compares all six as one grid first.

## 1. Locked palette

- Primary: deep transit-orange `#E8631A`
- Secondary/base: warm off-white `#F7F3EC`
- Accent (reserved, used sparingly): graphite `#2B2B2E`

## 2. Locked rendering technique

**Flat cut-paper negative-space cutout** — every tile rendered as if cut from a
single sheet of matte cardstock in one flat color per layer, with the motif read
entirely through the negative space cut out of the shape (no line art, no shading, no
gradient, no drop shadow) — the same technique held identical across all six tiles so
the comparison isolates concept, not rendering style.

## 3. Six distinct concepts

1. A wheel spoke pattern radiating from an off-center hub, suggesting motion without
   depicting a literal bike.
2. A folded paper street-map fold-line, suggesting routing/navigation.
3. A single continuous loop suggesting a helmet strap buckle, seen from above.
4. A dock-slot silhouette — the notch a bike locks into — repeated as a rhythm of
   three notches, suggesting the docking-station network.
5. A chain-link cutout, two links overlapping, suggesting connection between riders
   and stations rather than the mechanical chain itself.
6. A speed-line arc trailing off a single dot, suggesting a bike's path traced from
   above.

Each concept is genuinely distinct in form — a hub-and-spoke radial, a fold-line, a
loop, a repeated notch rhythm, an overlapping link pair, and an arc-and-dot — not six
palette-swapped versions of one bike silhouette.

## 4. The grid prompt

```
An icon exploration grid / concept comparison sheet for a bike-share app called
"Spokewise," arranged as a 2x3 grid of six square tiles over a warm off-white
#F7F3EC background, each tile evenly spaced with a thin graphite #2B2B2E dividing
line and a small caption beneath it. Every tile is rendered in the same flat
cut-paper negative-space cutout technique — a single sheet of matte deep
transit-orange #E8631A cardstock per tile, the motif read entirely through the
negative space cut out of the shape, no line art, no shading, no gradient, no drop
shadow. Tile 1, captioned "Spoke Radial": a wheel-spoke pattern radiating from an
off-center hub, suggesting motion without depicting a literal bicycle. Tile 2,
captioned "Fold Route": a folded paper street-map fold-line, suggesting routing and
navigation. Tile 3, captioned "Strap Loop": a single continuous loop suggesting a
helmet strap buckle seen from above. Tile 4, captioned "Dock Rhythm": a dock-slot
silhouette — the notch a bike locks into — repeated three times as a rhythm,
suggesting the docking-station network. Tile 5, captioned "Link Pair": two
overlapping chain-link cutouts, suggesting connection between riders and stations
rather than the mechanical chain itself. Tile 6, captioned "Trace Arc": a speed-line
arc trailing off a single dot, suggesting a bike's path traced from above. No
neural-network or circuit motifs, no glowing orb, no generic bicycle-wheel clip-art
silhouette repeated across tiles, no gradient bleeding across the sheet — every tile
stays a flat, deliberate cardstock cutout.
```

## 5. Handoff note

The reviewing team picked **Tile 4, "Dock Rhythm"** — the repeated dock-slot notch
read as the most ownable and the most specific to what Spokewise actually is (a
docking-station network, not a generic bike icon), where the other five leaned on
motifs any bike or transit brand could use. "Dock Rhythm" proceeds next as a
follow-up `brand-identity-sheet` generation, carrying forward the same locked
palette (`#E8631A` / `#F7F3EC` / `#2B2B2E`) and developing the dock-notch motif into
the full wordmark-card, app-icon-grid, and pill-button system. The other five
concepts are not carried forward as separate full boards.
